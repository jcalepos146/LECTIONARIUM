Dates follow the universal calendar. Many countries move Epiphany, Ascension and Corpus Christi to Sundays. Where a feast and a Sunday share a date, both are listed; which one is celebrated depends on each form's rules of precedence. St Joseph and the Annunciation are transferred when they fall in Holy Week or Easter Week. The Byzantine column uses the Gregorian Pascha (Melkite, Ruthenian in the Americas, Italo-Albanian and others). Churches that keep the Orthodox Paschalion, such as the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church in Ukraine, often keep Pascha on a later date. Its Luke Gospels follow the Slavic order, which differs from the Greek order on three Sundays. Before the Triodion, some weeks are filled according to the local ordo; these are marked "back-fill".

Reference The Catholic Church has 24 churches sui iuris: the Latin Church and 23 Eastern Catholic churches. Between them they use the lectionary systems below. Several Eastern churches share one liturgical tradition, so they are grouped by tradition.

Latin Church · Roman Rite

The three forms compared on the first tab, plus the Roman lectionary's adaptations.

Ordo Lectionum Missae

Roman Missal of Paul VI (the "Novus Ordo"); worldwide ordinary form
Book
1st edition 1969; 2nd edition 1981. Translated nationally (e.g. NABRE in the USA; ESV-CE in England and Wales from Advent 2024).
Cycle
Sundays: three years (A Matthew, B Mark with John 6, C Luke). Weekdays: two years for the first reading (I/II), one year for the Gospel.
Readings
Sundays and solemnities: Old Testament (Acts in Eastertide), responsorial psalm, Apostle, Gospel. Weekdays: one reading, psalm, Gospel.
Notes
OT reading chosen to harmonize with the Gospel; semicontinuous Epistles and Gospels in Ordinary Time. Its structure later inspired the Protestant Revised Common Lectionary (1992).
Status
In universal use

Missale Romanum 1962

Usus antiquior; FSSP, Institute of Christ the King, SSPX (irregular status), diocesan Latin Masses
Book
Missal of John XXIII under the 1960 Code of Rubrics.
Cycle
One year. Weekdays outside Lent and Ember days repeat the Sunday Mass unless a feast is kept.
Readings
Epistle (sometimes an OT lesson) and Gospel. Proper readings every day of Lent; extra lessons on Ember days; four prophecies at the Easter Vigil.
Notes
Gradual, Alleluia and Tract are psalm chants rather than readings.
Status
Restricted Traditionis custodes (2021) requires the bishop's permission, and Rome's approval for parish churches. Cardinal Roche said in July 2026 that Leo XIV will not reverse it. Leo XIV's March 2026 letter to the French bishops asked for "generous inclusion" of attached faithful.

Pre-1955 Roman Missal

Missal under the Divino Afflatu rubrics (1911–1954)
Book
Missale Romanum before Pius XII's Holy Week reform (1955) and the 1955 simplification of rubrics.
Cycle
One year, as 1962, with more vigils and octaves.
Readings
Same Sunday Epistles and Gospels as 1962. It differs in Holy Week (twelve Vigil prophecies, a lesson at the blessing of palms, full Passions), the Pentecost Vigil (six prophecies) and the proper Last Gospel of a displaced Sunday.
Status
No general permission Used mainly by groups outside full communion (e.g. SSPV, CMRI) and by some independent chapels. Within the Church, pre-1955 Holy Week has appeared only by occasional special permission.

Anglican-patrimony calendar: Sundays after Epiphany and after Trinity, the Septuagesima Sundays, Ember and Rogation days. The -gesima Sundays take Ordinary Time readings. The Compare tab shows these names under each Novus Ordo Sunday.

Eastern Catholic Churches

These lectionaries are built around their own liturgical years, so none of them maps onto Roman Sundays. Summaries only; parish practice varies.

Byzantine lectionary

Ukrainian, Ruthenian, Melkite, Romanian, Hungarian, Slovak, Italo-Albanian, Greek, Bulgarian, Križevci, Belarusian, Russian, Macedonian and Albanian Greek Catholics
Cycle
One year anchored to Pascha: John from Pascha to Pentecost, then Matthew, then Luke after the Elevation of the Cross (the "Lukan jump"), Mark on Lenten weekends. Fixed feasts take readings from the Menaion.
Readings
Apostol (Epistle or Acts) with prokeimenon, and Gospel. Old Testament readings (paremias) are read at Vespers, not at the Divine Liturgy.
Notes
Sundays are named for their Gospel: Publican and Pharisee, Prodigal Son, Zacchaeus, and others. The Compare tab gives its Sunday and great-feast readings for any year.

Maronite lectionary

Maronite Church (West Syriac/Antiochene)
Book
Revised Qurbono 1992; Lectionary 1993.
Cycle
One year. Seasons: Consecration and Renewal of the Church · Glorious Birth · Epiphany (with the Commemorations of Priests, the Righteous and the Faithful Departed) · Great Lent (from Cana Sunday) · Resurrection · Pentecost · Glorious Cross.
Readings
Epistle (or Acts or Revelation) and Gospel. An Old Testament reading, discussed since the 2003–2006 Patriarchal Synod, has not been introduced.

West Syriac lectionary

Syriac Catholic Church; Syro-Malankara Catholic Church
Cycle
Year opens with the Sundays of Consecration and Dedication of the Church (Qudosh Ito, Hudos Ito) in late October and November.
Readings
Traditionally Old Testament lessons, then Acts or a Catholic Epistle, a Pauline Epistle and the Gospel. Parishes often use a shortened set.

East Syriac lectionary

Syro-Malabar Church; Chaldean Catholic Church
Cycle
Nine seasons: Annunciation (Subara), Nativity, Epiphany (Denha), Great Fast, Resurrection, Apostles, Summer (Qaita), Elijah–Cross–Moses, Dedication of the Church. The first and last seasons have four weeks; the others have seven symbolic weeks.
Readings
Old Testament readings (Law and Prophets), Epistle, and Gospel at the Holy Qurbana and Raza.

Armenian lectionary

Armenian Catholic Church
Cycle
Armenian liturgical year, descended from the ancient lectionary of Jerusalem.
Readings
Old Testament prophecy (on many days), Epistle and Gospel, with psalm verses (saghmos).

Katameros (Alexandrian)

Coptic Catholic Church
Cycle
Coptic year (from Nayrouz, September), with a separate Lenten and Paschal Katameros.
Readings
Pauline Epistle, Catholic Epistle, Acts (Praxis), psalm, Gospel.

Ge'ez lectionary

Ethiopian and Eritrean Catholic Churches
Cycle
Ethiopian calendar, Alexandrian in origin.
Readings
Pauline Epistle, Catholic Epistle, Acts, psalm and Gospel.

Reference · proposal A proposal, not a liturgical book. It keeps the traditional calendar and its one-year Epistles and Gospels, and adds the wider Old Testament reading that the 1969 reform made possible. The added readings are taken from the post-conciliar lectionary, mostly where it already pairs them with the same Gospel. Where the pre-1955 books read more Scripture than 1962, their readings return.

Principles

  • The traditional core stays. The calendar (Septuagesima, Passiontide, the Pentecost octave, Ember days, Sundays after Pentecost) and every Sunday Epistle and Gospel stay exactly as in 1962. A congregation hears the same Gospel on the same Sunday every year, as Christians in the West did for more than a millennium.
  • Every Sunday gains a Prophecy. An Old Testament reading is read before the Epistle, as in the Ambrosian and Mozarabic rites. It is chosen so that it explains the Gospel, and it is the reading the new lectionary itself pairs with that Gospel or its synoptic parallel wherever one exists.
  • Where a lesson already stands, the Apostle is added. Feasts whose first reading is already from the Old Testament or Acts (Epiphany, Ascension, Pentecost, many saints' days) keep it and gain the new lectionary's New Testament reading.
  • Paschaltide reads Acts. From Easter to Pentecost the added reading comes from Acts, following the new lectionary and the ancient practice described by St Augustine.
  • Holy Week and the vigils return to their pre-1955 fullness. The twelve prophecies of the Easter Vigil, the six of the Pentecost Vigil, the lesson at the blessing of palms and the full Matthew Passion are restored. Good Friday adds the fourth Servant Song (Isa 52:13–53:12), which is missing from both older forms.
  • The psalm is sung, not read. The Gradual, Alleluia and Tract remain the psalmody between readings; no responsorial psalm is added.
  • The proper Last Gospel returns. When a feast displaces a Sunday, that Sunday's Gospel is read at the end of Mass, as before 1960, so no Sunday Gospel is lost in a given year.

Participation and canonical guidance

The first time a Eucharistic liturgy is opened, the site asks whether you are a priest or studying as a seminarian. Lay study is the default: every liturgy in the library is shown without its words of consecration (the institution narrative and, in the Eastern rites, the invocation over the gifts), which are replaced by a short notice. Priest or seminarian mode shows them, with this warning: simulating the words of consecration, or any other sacrament, is a grave offence under canon law (cf. can. 1379), and doing so in a public setting gives scandal; the texts are for study and for supervised practice in priestly formation. The choice is remembered on this device and can be changed with the Participation setting above each liturgy. The Reformed orders are shown in full in either mode, because their words of institution are read as a warrant from Scripture rather than said as a prayer of consecration. A live host cannot change another participant’s local priest mode. Priest-only prayers and actions are not lay assigned parts; the read-aloud tool does not recite priest-only Mass prayers.

Canons 900 §1 and 907: only a validly ordained priest can confect the Eucharist; during the Eucharistic celebration, deacons and lay people do not offer the prayers or perform actions proper to the celebrating priest. Canon 1379 §1, 1° concerns a non-priest attempting the liturgical celebration of the Eucharistic Sacrifice. Studying a historical text is distinct from attempting that celebration.

Canon 276 §2 addresses clerical holiness: faithful pastoral ministry, Scripture and the Eucharist, the daily Hours according to proper approved books, retreats, mental prayer, confession and Marian devotion. Priests and deacons preparing for priesthood have the daily Hours obligation; permanent deacons follow the extent defined by their bishops’ conference. A selected, shortened or historical guide here does not certify fulfillment of that obligation.

These citations are from the Latin Code of Canon Law. Eastern Catholic practice also requires its own applicable law and approved books. Priest or seminarian mode is a local declaration, not verification of ordination, faculties or permission to celebrate.

Known limits

  • The Byzantine column is tabulated. The other Eastern lectionaries are described only, because their years don't align with Roman Sundays.
  • Byzantine back-fill weeks before the Triodion, and the months when the breviary skips a week of its monthly course, are approximated; a printed ordo decides.
  • In Compare the year, every column applies feast precedence for the major fixed feasts (transfers, feasts replacing Sundays, Byzantine feasts kept with the Sunday). The traditional columns keep a displaced Sunday as its own row, marked as commemorated; the Pray today guide applies the full 1962 and pre-1955 rules to every day.
  • The Novus Ordo guide follows the General Roman Calendar with the proper calendar of the United States, including obligatory and optional memorials; other countries' calendars differ.
  • The daily guide covers 30 November 2025 to 30 November 2030.
  • The Liturgy of the Hours psalmody follows the four-week psalter; solemnities, feasts and the Easter Octave have proper psalms, which are not substituted. The Douay-Rheims wording differs from the Grail and Abbey Psalms of the official books.
  • The Byzantine Divine Liturgy itself is given as an order with the readings: its full text needs a public-domain source (such as Hapgood's Service Book, 1906) that has not yet been added.
  • Divine Worship: Daily Office adapts the 1662 orders in places (the suffrages, the prayers after the collects, some proper collects); the 1662 text is given, so follow the Divine Worship book where it differs. Its lessons are cued, not printed.
  • The Hybrid tab describes the weekday plan; the day-by-day hybrid weekday readings are in the Pray today guide.
  • Postures follow common United States customs and vary by parish and country. Office hour lengths are counted from the full text where it is given and estimated from the order of each hour elsewhere.
  • Session lengths are estimates: spoken text at about 140 words a minute, chant and hymns from their notation, fixed allowances for silent prayers and Communion. Timed (shortened) sessions are study aids; they do not authorize a liturgical celebration or fulfill a cleric’s obligation to pray the approved Office.
